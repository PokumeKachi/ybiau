# Story Of My Life

Greetings, my name is Nguyen Cao Long Khanh. I am currently a HCMUS student majoring in APCS. Before I tell my story, here are a few things you should know about me. Before entering 3rd grade, I was a studious little dork who was constantly bullied at school and annoyed by his older sister at home, a Flash game enthusiast who was barely allowed any screen time, a quiet kid at school who was raised too well to go around picking fights with others, a 7-year-old child living with two straight-laced teachers who loved their son and hoped that he would excel at Maths and other Natural Science subjects.

My story (and my humble achievements) began when I was in 3rd grade, which, prior to Vietnam’s 2018 Curricular Reforms, marked an 8-year-old’s introduction to English and IT, in both of which I gradually accumulated experience.

I graduated primary school being academically competent just barely enough to single me out as a local celebrity with national medals in IOE, and also enough to make me complacent with my own lack of knowledge. Shortly afterwards, the whole world was hit by the COVID-19 pandemic, and being stuck at home, I had the chance to take a deeper dive into the global world of technology on my family’s TV. The clunky old device barely had anything to offer but a poorly adapted interface of YouTube and a web browser that barely supported JavaScript, but it was the precious portal through which I learned about video games (specifically Pokémon, Undertale, and Minecraft). While this may appear as a total contrast to the academically obsessed nerd that was 3rd-grade me, it was a natural change for any teenage boys back in my age.

The idea of making games had lingered since I was nine, when I researched ways to upload Flash games to 24h Games for money. In 6th grade, discovering fan-made Pokémon games reignited this passion. So I did research on modding tools, game development tools, and accidentally installed viruses onto my parents’ laptops twice in the process. Also around this same time, I read my older sister's 8th-grade IT textbook, and honestly I was appalled by the interface of the Pascal IDE at first (even now I still think it was a terrible pick for the IT curriculum back then), but I was excited by the idea of being able to control the computer (I was thinking of ways I could cheat through tests using it, e.g. instead of coming up with the formula for summing all natural numbers from 1 to n, we could just write a for-loop program!).

Then, 7th grade came around, and it was time for my school’s selection exam for the Gifted Students’ Competition. I was dead set on getting into the Maths team, which was my dad's major. Unfortunately, I was not good with the kind of theory-and-proof Maths that they tested us on in the exams. My dad was disappointed, and seeing me tinkering with the computer only made him more frustrated, because that was a time when video games were seen as an addiction (which I now have to admit they might be in some cases). He scolded me, tried to teach me, and had me transferred to Ben Hai Secondary and High School, where he worked as one of the vice principals.

And so began my 8th grade – with all the stress, expectations, and failures, I became a rebellious adolescent boy crazily obsessed with gaming. I opened my first YouTube channel, which required even more screen time to maintain. And so I started begging my parents for more computer usage, to which they responded by making me do test papers, and letting me play games if I got good enough scores. One day, I was given the National 12th Grade Graduation exam paper to solve. The subject was English, and mom promised me 3 hours of playtime if I got a flawless 10, and 2 if I got 9. I could manage an 8, and barely secure a 9, but being the greedy kid that I was, I slammed my door shut and locked it, and luckily, my dad had forgotten his phone inside the bedroom, which I knew the password to by peeking at the screen any time I could. And the good thing about the National 12th Grade Graduation exam is that it is such a big event that its answer keys are always widely available online – at least widely enough for 13-year-old me to look them up. My mom was taken aback when she saw my scores, thus she started frantically and excitedly discussing finding me a proper English teacher while I played Roblox on the family desktop.

That was how I smugly entered 9th grade with a Provincial Second Prize in English against contestants who were mostly one year older than me. Of course, the journey to get there was much more tiring, as I was worn out by the workload given to me at the time by Mr. Minh Luu, the impeccably proper English teacher that my mom found for me, whom I still admire to this day. Hence, I was brought back into Maths, now once again up against my peers. March 15th, 2023 marked my first time scoring less than 25% (4.75/20) on an official Maths test.

My dad passed away 22 days later. I heard the news at noon, after having come back from the IOE national round at my school. It shattered my family, my school, my neighborhood, and my entire world. For the first time, I was less protected, less sheltered, less assured of myself. And so, I finished secondary school and entered high school. Mr. Anh Quang, a prominent IT teacher at Vinh Linh High School, impressed by my entry project for the provincial Young IT Competition (Tin học Trẻ), which was a keyboard typing game made in Godot, invited me to join the Gifted Students Team of IT. That’s when I started my 4-month rush to learn algorithms and problem-solving, which I did a sloppy job at and only got the provincial Consolation Prize in the Gifted Students’ Competition.

Then my sister, a brand-new undergraduate medical major, was found dead two days after Christmas. Once again, reality tore my rose-tinted world apart, took a piece of it away, and reminded me of the fragility of precious life. But then again, I had no choice but to forge ahead and persevere. And so my 10th grade ended with a provincial second prize in STEM (Khoa học Kỹ thuật).

As I began 11th grade, I dove right back into English, my main strength as soon as summer arrived. After a long time, First Prize was finally within reach, and First Prize I did win. Even more surprisingly, I made it narrowly into the team for the National English Competition. Thus began my 3-month venture into Quang Tri’s Le Quy Don Gifted High School in preparation for the competition. There, I had new friends, new promises, new feelings. And I won Consolation Prize, further securing a slot at my dream university (which… funnily enough, I still had no idea which university it was at the time).

Right at the start of the summer of 12th grade, I, being the challenge-loving kid that I am, jumped into Physics. While I was preparing for what would eventually result in another provincial second prize, the APCS program at HCMUS also caught my attention with its impossibly high entry scores. So I saw it as the end goal, and started scrambling for every chance I had to secure my entry – V-ACT, IELTS, you name it.

Back to now, it is August 28th, 2026. I have officially and legally (under Vietnamese law) become an adult for exactly 20 days. The next four years of mine are set on APCS itself, and despite industry concerns about AI replacing programmers, I remain steadfast in my commitment to FOSS developers, who create the toolsets that many of us unknowingly rely on, and I hope to eventually become one of them. At the same time, I am worried about whether I can carry the burden of studying Computer Science, Physics, and Maths all at the same time while residing a thousand kilometres away from home. I know they are my true interests, and that I want to learn more of the world, but looking at the tuition fees, I can’t help but regret that knowledge has become a commodity as I exit my teenage years in pursuit of higher education.

Therefore, I am writing this essay to apply for the Dinh Thien Ly scholarship of the year 2026–2027, which, if awarded to me, will greatly lighten the financial burden upon my mom, the only close family member who is here to support me right now. Furthermore, my incentive to contribute to community software projects will be further strengthened. As a little postscript, I would like to thank the organizing body for providing support to students in need, and I hope such financial aid will only grow in number from now on. Thank you for reading.
